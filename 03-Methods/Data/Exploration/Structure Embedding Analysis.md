|Representation|Key components / what is encoded|Pros|Cons|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Atom coordinates / full 3D structure**|3D Cartesian coordinates of all (or many) atoms; maybe bond edges; full backbone + side chains|Very rich; can capture fine geometric detail; good for tasks sensitive to fine structure (binding, active sites)|Large (many atoms), high variability; coordinate system issues (need invariance to rotation/translation); expensive; possibly noisy for side chains.|
|**Residue‐level graphs (Cα or Cβ atoms)**|Nodes = residues, with e.g. the position of the Cα (or Cβ) atom; edges based on distance threshold (e.g. if two residues are within x Å), or contact maps, or maybe edges for sequential adjacency + spatial adjacency.|Lower complexity; still captures tertiary contacts; often good enough for many tasks; smaller model.|Loses side-chain detail; may not capture subtle geometry; edge decisions (thresholds) can be arbitrary.|
|**Contact maps / distance matrices**|Matrix of pairwise distances (or binary “contacts”) between residues (or selected atoms); sometimes distances truncated / discretised.|Simple; rotational/translational invariance; easy to input into e.g. CNNs; summarizes global structure.|Loses which atom; threshold choice matters; may compress away detail; matrix size grows with length.|
|**Angles / dihedral / torsion features**|Backbone torsion angles (phi, psi, omega), maybe side chain angles; sometimes pairwise angles; maybe also dihedral‐angle based local conformation info.|Encodes local geometry; rotation‐invariant (angles don’t change under rigid motion); smaller dimension; good for capturing local backbone structure.|Doesn’t directly encode global folding; long‐range interactions still need graph or distances; side chain conformation sometimes missing.|
|**Secondary structure / structural alphabets**|For each residue: label of secondary structure (helix, sheet, coil, etc.), possibly along with solvent accessibility, backbone exposure, etc.; or even structural alphabet motifs (small repeating local motifs).|Compact; often informative; robust; many proteins’ functions depend on secondary structure.|Coarse; many details lost; doesn’t capture 3D orientations beyond coarse categories.|
|**Physico‐chemical / environmental features**|For nodes: amino acid type, charge, hydrophobicity; solvent accessibility; local environment (neighbors within some radius); for edges: spatial distance, orientation, whether contacts are hydrogen bond, etc.|Augments purely geometrical representation; often improves prediction especially when combined with coordinate info.|Requires some preprocessing; may require generating solvent accessibility or other computed features; can introduce noise.|
|**Graph representations**|Combine nodes & edges as above: e.g. residue graphs (nodes = residues), edges = sequence adjacency + spatial adjacency; node features = whatever residue / local geometry; sometimes edge features carry geometry (distance, direction, angle)|Very flexible; GNNs can naturally use this; many recent works follow this.|Needs design: what’s a “good” graph? edges, features; can be computationally heavy for large proteins; balancing local vs global info is non‐trivial.|
